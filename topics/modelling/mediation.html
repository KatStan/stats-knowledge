mediation

What question does this answer?

How does an exposure affect an outcome via an intermediate variable? Does the effect of exposure to outcome change now that we’ve introduced another variable in the mix that the exposure does have an effect on? Causal inference by finding an underlying mechanism under effect of the exposure, and contributing to the outcome (be wary or saying something causes something, experimental setup is critical there). A mediator explains the underlying mechanism of the relationship between the exposure and the outcome.

What is it doing?

We have an exposure that has an effect (at least theoretically a relationship) with the outcome, and we have another variable that the exposure has an effect on generally (remember to see assumptions below in Section 3!) This other variable we’ll call the mediator. Effectively, we care about whether there is a difference in the exposure’s effect on the outcome now that we have introduced the mediator, or not.

When can I use it?

  • There is a relationship between exposure and outcome (even if the regression doesn’t return significance, if there is a good theoretical reasoning, that’s enough). Without a relationship, there is nothing to mediate
  • There is a relationship between exposure and mediator, as is the idea that the exposure affects the mediator that affects the outcome
  • The outcome is binary -> not a requirement, just a package(s) and result interpretation convenience
  • Assumptions:
    1. Must control for exposure-outcome confounding (i.e. include all covariates)
    2. Must control for mediator-outcome confounding
    3. Must control for exposure-mediator confounding
    4. The exposure must not be affecting any of the mediator-outcome confounders (o/w C2 would be a mediator of E -> Y and is hence confounding the effect of M on Y)

Note #1: In practice, some of the covariates may affect all of E, M and Y, and the covariates may also affect each other. None of this is problematic and the covariate groups C1, C2, and C3 need not be distinguished from one another. What is important is that the covariates included in the regression models above suffice to control for exposure-outcome, mediator-outcome, and exposure-mediator confounding

Note #2: Another way to think about the fourth assumption is that there should be relatively little time between the exposure and the mediator. If a substantial gap exists, then the fourth assumption requires that nothing on the pathway from the exposure to the mediator itself also independently affects the outcome. This assumption likely becomes increasingly less plausible the more time that elapses between the exposure and the mediator

Reporting

  • Total effect/TE is the effect of X on Y without M (Y~X)
  • Direct effect/NDE/ADE is the effect of X on Y with M (Y~X+M)
  • Indirect effect/NIE/ACME is total minus direct, and what you want to see whether it is significant, as that answers the question of whether X works through M to effect Y, i.e. whether M affects Y, but X no longer affects Y as much, now that M is there.
  • Mediation
  • Medlfex

Since the sequential ignorability assumption is untestable, we recommend that the researchers conduct a sensitivity analysis via the medsens function, which can be applied to certain statistical models - TBA In practice, you won’t be 100% sure of whether you have included all confounders, so sensitivity analyses to test for robustness of the in/direct estimates are necessary

  • You can have the mediator model and the outcome model have different covariates (as in, you can add more covariates to the outcome model)
set.seed(2014)
data("framing", package = "mediation")

# The data captures subjects that were exposed to different media stories about 
# immigration and the authors investigated how their framing influences
# attitudes and political behavior regarding immigration policy. They propose anxiety
# as the mediating variable for the causal effect of framing on public opinion. 
# We first fit the mediator model where the measure of anxiety (emo) is modeled as
# a function of the framing treatment (treat - binary) and pre-treatment covariates (age, 
# educ, gender, and income). Next, we model the outcome variable, which is a binary
# variable indicating whether or not the participant agreed to send a letter about
# immigration policy to his or her member of Congress (cong_mesg - binary). The 
# explanatory variables of the outcome model include the mediator, treatment status,
# and the same set of pre-treatment variables as those used in the mediator model.
# In this example, the treatment is expected to increase the level of respondents'
# emotional response, which in turn is hypothesized to make subjects more likely 
# to send a letter to his or her member of Congress. 

med.fit <- lm(emo ~ treat + age + educ + gender + income, data = framing)
out.fit <- glm(cong_mesg ~ emo + treat + age + educ + gender + income,
                          data = framing, family = binomial("probit"))

# In general, as long as computing power is not an issue, analysts should
# estimate confidence intervals via the bootstrap (boot=TRUE) with more than 1000 
# resamples, which is the default number of simulations (sims)
# The bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) intervals has better asymptotic properties 
# and is often recommended for the estimation of mediation effects (Preacher and Hayes 2008)
med.out <- mediation::mediate(med.fit, 
                   out.fit, 
                   treat = "treat", # treat is short for treatment aka exposure
                   mediator = "emo",
                   boot = TRUE,
                   boot.ci.type = "bca",
                   sims = 1000)

In this example, the estimated ACMEs are statistically significantly different from zero. The results suggest that the treatment in the framing experiment may have increased emotional response, which in turn made subjects more likely to send a message to his or her member of Congress. Here, since the outcome is binary, all estimated effects are expressed as the increase in probability that the subject sent a message to his or her Congress person.

The ACME is the estimated average increase in the dependent variable on average that arrives as a result of the mediators rather than ‘directly’ from the treatment. The ACME (control) is the estimated average increase in the dependent variable among the control group hat arrives as a result of the mediators rather than ‘directly’ from the treatment.

If the outcome (and mediator) is continuous, ACME is in units of the outcome and interpreting it is like for a standard linear regression (if data was scaled, then it is in standard deviations, not 1-unit changes).
So if we had here 0.083 for ACME and the outcome was continuous, and we scaled at least the exposure and outcome, it would be interpreted as a 1-standard-deviation increase in the exposure, then the pathway through the mediator “causes’ the outcome to change by 0.083 standard deviations on average (rather than through ‘just’ the exposure).
If it’s just the exposure that’s scaled, a 1-standard-deviation increase in the exposure, then the pathway through the mediator causes the outcome to change by 0.083 raw units.
If it were only the outcome that was scaled, a 1-unit increase in the exposure would lead to the pathway through the mediator causing the outcome to change by 0.083 standard deviations.

This is the average effect for “everyone”, ignoring individual covariate differences.

set.seed(2014)
data("UPBdata", package = "medflex")

# The data is a subsample of 385 individuals from a survey study that involved the
# recruitment of individuals who divorced between March 2008 and March 2009 in
# four major courts in Flanders.
# It aimed to improve the quality of life in families during and after the divorce 
# by translating research findings into practical guidelines for separation 
# specialists (such as lawyers, judges, psychologists, welfare workers...) and by
# promoting evidence-based policy. The corresponding dataset (UPBdata) involves 
# those who responded to a battery of questionnaires related to romantic relationship 
# characteristics (such as adult attachment style) and breakup characteristics 
# (such as breakup initiator status, experiencing negative affectivity and 
# engaging in unwanted pursuit behaviors; UPB) (DeSmet, Loeys, and Buysse 2012). 
# Respondents were asked to imagine their former partner as well as possible and 
# to remember how they generally felt in their relationship before the breakup 
# when completing the attachment style questionnaire. The mediation hypothesis of
# interest concerned the question whether the level of emotional distress or 
# negative affectivity experienced during the breakup can be regarded as an 
# intermediate mechanism (M) through which attachment style towards the ex-partner
# before the breakup (X) exerts its influence on displaying UPBs after the breakup
# (Y) 

# As direct and indirect effects are most easily understood for a binary exposure, 
# we will use a dichotomized version of anxious attachment level (attbin) for 
# didactic purposes. Moreover, negative affectivity (negaff) has been standardized 
# (sd=1) to allow for easily interpretable effect estimates. The original att has 
# been scaled. The outcome variable unwanted pursuit behavior (UPB) indicates 
# whether (=1) or not (=0) the respondent has engaged in any unwanted pursuit 
#behaviors.

medFit <- glm(negaff ~ factor(attbin) + gender + educ + age,
              family = gaussian, 
              data = UPBdata)

expData <- medflex::neWeight(medFit)

A counterfactual model reporting:
Exponentiating the model parameter estimates provides estimates that can be interpreted as odds ratios. For instance, for a subject with baseline covariate levels C, altering the level of anxious attachment from low (=0) to high (=1), while controlling negative affectivity at levels as naturally observed at any given level of anxious attachment x, increases the odds of displaying unwanted pursuit behaviors with a factor \(e^{\beta_1} = e^{0.3959} = 1.49\) (natural direct effect)

Altering levels of negative affectivity as observed at low anxious attachment scores to levels that would have been observed at high anxious attachment scores, while controlling their anxious attachment score at any given level x, increases the odds of displaying unwanted pursuit behaviors with a factor \(e^{\beta_2} = e^{0.352} = 1.42\) (natural indirect effect).

# we can have continuous exposures, where instead of enumerating all
# exposure levels to construct auxiliary variables x and x∗
# for each subject, Vansteelandt et al. (2012b) proposed to draw specific quantiles
# from the conditional density of the exposure given baseline covariates. 
# By default, these hypothetical exposure levels are drawn from a linear
# model for the exposure, conditional on a linear combination of all covariates 
# specified in the working model. 
# If one wishes to use another model for the exposure, this default model 
# specification can be overruled by referring to a fitted model object in the xFit
# argument. Misspecification of this sampling model does not induce bias in the 
# estimated coefficients and standard errors of the natural effect model

# Both neWeight and neImpute allow to choose the number of draws to sample from this
# conditional density via the nRep argument (which defaults to 5), simulations 
# have shown the gain to be ignorable when choosing more than 5 draws 

expData_cont_exp <- medflex::neWeight(negaff ~ att + gender + educ + age,
                    family = gaussian, 
                    data = UPBdata, 
                    nRep = 5)

neMod_cont_exp <- medflex::neModel(UPB ~ att0 + att1 + gender + educ + age,
                          family = binomial("logit"),

If the outcome was continuous, it would be interpreted the same as it was in the mediation package, only with the caveat that the output medflex gives is conditional on baseline covariate levels C. The difference between conditional and marginal is negligible if there is no interaction between the variables. medflex handles interactions in models TBA.
